1. Reflection Q1 - I believe that the author's main points are that
there is a disparity between what AI's capabilities and it's perceived capabilities,
there is insufficient accountability for the consequences of AI use (e.g. the hackings etc.),
and that AI is a complex tool that should be pushing us towards thoughtful interaction,
not towards a hands-off lifestyle where we stop thinking about the context of our situations.

2. Reflection Q2 - The author claims that AI is a valuable tool, but also one whose value may be overstated.
Despite being tauted as the ultimate penny saver there are more than the surface level costs at play.
Sometimes a product requires guarantees, and the author claims that no matter how good your environment
or safety nets are, AI cannot always guarantee success. Software development is a complex process
and, at least for now, one that needs to involve humans thinking critically about things like what the problem is,
why this needs addressed, alternative solutions, and so on.

3. Reflection Q3 - I really like that the author advocates for responsibility and accountability for AI.
This seems like a no-brainer to me, and quite frankly I find it a little unsettling that there hasn't been more of a push.
AI is a tool, and one that many use in many contexts. Just as there is responsibility behind the relatively complex process of
driving a car, I feel that there should be accountability for AI. I also appreciated the perspective the
author provides on the potential ubiquity of AI. If AI becomes so good that it truly can do almost whatever you want just from a prompt,
then the people that simply take AI output will be producing simply average work. To create
quality work you need to be engaging intelligently and learning how to use the tools to do something you
couldn't do before AND that people can't easily do now.

4. Reflection Q4 - One question I would have is how the author would suggest implementing accountability for AI.
Clearly the author has more experience with AI and software development than I do, so I was curious.
Despite my belief that the should be more accountability, a simple question someone might have would be
"won't this slow down or stunt the development of AI?" While I think that question is less important
to think about than if we need accountability in an age where AI is also being used to plan mass shootings,
hack private information, and has very real odds to end the world according to some leaders at AI companies,
it is a question that would likely get brough up in the never-ending effort to increase shareholder value.
